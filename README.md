## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

This is the path a submission takes:
1. **Submit the text:** The user sends a text and a `creator_id` to the `/submit` endpoint through a POST request.
2. **Validate the input:** The system checks that the request contains the required information before processing the text.
3. **Analyze the text with the model:** The `detector.py` module analyzes how predictable the text is and produces a `model_score`.
4. **Analyze the writing style:** The `stylometry.py` module examines writing patterns, such as sentence structure and word usage, and produces a `style_score`.
5. **Combine the results:** The `scoring.py` module combines both signals to calculate a final confidence score.
6. **Assign a label:** Based on the combined score and the defined thresholds, the system assigns one of three labels: high-confidence human, unsure, or high-confidence AI.
7. **Record and return the result:** The system assigns a unique `content_id`, saves the decision in the audit log, and returns a JSON response containing the classification, confidence score, label, and both signal scores.

### API Contracts
1. POST /submit
This endpoint receives a text and analyzes whether it might be AI-generated.
Input: text (the writing to analyze) and creator_id (who submitted it).
Output: content_id (unique text ID), guess (estimated classification), confidence (combined score), label (message for the user), model_score (model result), and style_score (writing style result).

2. POST /appeal
This endpoint allows a person to request a review of a result.
Input: content_id (which text is being appealed) and reasoning (why the person disagrees).
Output: content_id, status (under_review), and message (confirmation that the appeal was recorded).
The system should check that the content exists before accepting an appeal. An appeal does not automatically change the original result.

### False Positive Scenario
A student writes an essay in English using simple vocabulary and repetitive expressions because English is not her first language. The system incorrectly identifies her writing as potentially AI-generated.
For this example, the system assigns a hypothetical score of 0.92, indicating a high likelihood of AI-generated writing according to its scoring method.
Instead of accusing the student, the system displays this message:
"This text shows patterns potentially associated with AI-generated content. This result is not definitive."
The student can submit an appeal explaining that she wrote the essay herself and providing additional information, such as earlier drafts.
The system records the appeal using the document's unique `content_id` and changes its status to `under_review`. The original classification is not automatically changed.
This example shows why the system needs carefully chosen thresholds and an appeal process. A false positive can unfairly affect a person who wrote their own work.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:**
The model analyzes how predictable the text is based on language patterns. It produces a score that helps estimate whether the text might have been generated by AI.
**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:**
AI-generated text may follow more predictable language patterns, while human writing can include more variation in word choices and expressions.
**What it can't see:**
The model cannot determine with certainty who wrote the text. For example, a person whose first language is not English might use simple vocabulary, repetitive words, or formal expressions. The model could incorrectly identify this writing as AI-generated.
Also, AI-generated text that has been significantly edited by a person might be more difficult for the model to identify.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:**
This signal analyzes writing characteristics, such as sentence length, vocabulary variety, and repetitive patterns.
**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:**
Human and AI-generated texts may have different writing patterns. For example, AI-generated text might use more consistent sentence structures or repetitive expressions.
**What it can't see:**
Writing style alone cannot prove whether a text was written by AI or a person. Someone might naturally write in a formal or repetitive way, especially when English is not their first language.
Also, a person could modify AI-generated text by changing words or sentence structures, making it more difficult for this signal to recognize the original patterns.

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:**
The system combines two signals to estimate whether a text shows patterns associated with AI-generated writing. The model signal receives more weight because our initial tests showed that the style signal was less reliable for short texts.

When the two signals strongly disagree, the system returns a combined score of 0.50 to represent uncertainty and reduce the risk of falsely classifying human-written text as AI generated.

**The numbers:**
Model signal weight: 0.70 (70%)
Style signal weight: 0.30 (30%)
Combined score = (0.70 * model_score) + (0.30 * style_score)
Strong disagreement: one signal >= 0.80 and the other <= 0.20.
Strong disagreement result: combined score = 0.50.

**Where it lives:** 
scoring.py::combine_signals (weights and disagreement thresholds in config.py).


<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**
The clearly human-written text received a model score of 0.2340 and a style score of 0.6410. The difference between the signals was 0.4070.
Because the scores did not meet the strong disagreement thresholds, the system used the weighted average and returned a combined score of 0.3561.
This example shows that the style signal may assign a relatively high AI-related score to human writing, especially when the text is short.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00–0.35 | This text looks more like human writing. However, no automated tool can confirm authorship with certainty. |
| unsure | Greater than 0.35 and less than 0.65 | We couldn't determine whether this text was written by a person or with AI. Writing styles vary, and this result does not indicate that you did anything wrong. You do not need to change your writing based on this result. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.65–1.00 | This text has some patterns commonly found in AI-generated writing. This doesn't necessarily mean AI was used. If you wrote this yourself, you can request a review. |

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**

I chose this wording because I want users to understand that an uncertain result is not an accusation. Writing styles vary, and the system cannot always distinguish between human and AI-generated text. The message should be clear, respectful, and reassuring, especially for people who wrote the text themselves.

<!-- It's the hardest of the three. It has to admit uncertainty without
     sounding like an accusation, because the person reading it may well have
     written every word themselves. -->

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** 10 per minute, 200 per day

**Why those numbers and not others:**
I chose 10 requests per minute and 200 requests per day to balance usability and protection against automated abuse. A legitimate writer may need to submit multiple versions of a document, but is unlikely to require more than 10 submissions in a single minute.

The daily limit allows repeated use while restricting the total number of requests from one caller. These limits do not completely prevent automated attacks, but they reduce the speed at which someone can test variations to identify weaknesses in the detector.

For a production environment, I would review actual usage patterns and adjust the limits to balance security and usability.

<!-- Think about two people: a real writer submitting their own work a few
     times an hour, and a script sending a thousand variations to map your
     thresholds. Your numbers have to be liveable for the first and hostile to
     the second. -->

**What a caller counts as:** <!-- per address, or per creator_id? They fail
differently — one script can look like a thousand callers, and one household
can look like one. -->

The rate limiter uses `creator_id` to identify a caller when a valid, non-empty string is provided. If `creator_id` is missing or invalid, it falls back to the caller's IP address.
This approach allows different creators sharing the same IP address to have separate rate limits. However, an attacker could potentially bypass the limit by submitting requests with different creator IDs, since the application does not authenticate those identifiers. The IP fallback also means that users without a valid creator ID who share the same network may share a rate limit.
